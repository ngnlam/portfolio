<Section id="problem" title="A wish list, not a requirement">

VCS listed tasks it hoped a security robot could take over. Engineering needed to know which tasks to build first, what exactly Dyno must detect, and who acts on an alert. With a vague brief, months of perception work could go into the wrong thing.

<Section id="found" title="The real job happens on a scooter, at walking pace">

1. **The guard's real round.** A guard rides the basement on an electric scooter at walking pace, about 90 minutes a round, then goes back to the desk to check plates and rewind camera footage. One guard covers more than a thousand parking spaces. Dyno's value is spotting and alerting during the round, so nobody has to rebuild it from footage afterward.
2. **A hard limit on the robot.** The battery lasts about two hours and a round takes 90 minutes. That one fact decides the operating model, so I put it at the top of the open questions.
3. **Three questions decide what happens to an object.** The brief sorted items by where they were. On site, the guards' choice came down to three questions: does it belong to someone, can one person carry it, and is it hazardous. Each answer leads somewhere different: the nearest bin, a safe spot and a call to housekeeping, the contractor who left it, or the lost property desk.
4. **Evidence comes before touching.** Guards had been caught in disputes when an owner claimed something was missing. So before anyone picks up someone's belongings, three things must be true: the spot is on camera, a witness is present, and the contents are filmed.
5. **A guard's job doesn't end at reporting.** They hand the problem to the right team with a deadline, then come back about 15 minutes later to check it's done. A robot that only sends alerts would skip the part guards care about.
6. **A cheaper signal for charging violations.** The first idea was to connect to the chargers' live data. The color of the charger's LED gives the same signal with no integration.

<Section id="decisions" title="Four calls that shaped the spec">

<Decisions>
<Decision title="Describe today's job before the robot's">

Every BRD opens with how the work is done now: outcome states first, each phase as an actor and action table, corner cases as signal and action pairs. I turned this into the template the team used for every use case.

</Decision>
<Decision title="Cut stories until each one changes what gets built">

The parking requirement went from 18 user stories to 13, then 6, grouped by the life of an alert: capture, decision, uncertainty, handoff, tracking and closing, recording.

</Decision>
<Decision title="Leave decisions with people">

I ruled out four requests and wrote the reason next to each: reminding drivers still in their cars (no reliable way to detect it), Dyno calling reception itself (guards must be able to overrule a false alert), tracing owners through CCTV (the camera system already does it), and deciding what to do about a violation (the guard's call).

</Decision>
<Decision title="Label targets as assumptions">

I proposed thresholds, such as detection rates by violation type and alerts reaching the guard's app within 10 seconds, and marked each as an assumption to confirm with users. Engineering got a starting point without mistaking it for a promise.

</Decision>
</Decisions>

</Section>

<Section id="shipped" title="Two BRDs, one spec, one shared template">

- Two BRDs, parking violations and trash or flammable items.
- A requirement spec for parking violations: 13 scope lines, 7 violation types, 10 alert fields, 8 open items, 21 acceptance criteria.
- The BRD framework and template the team used across use cases.
- Flow diagrams, embedded in the team's combined BRD at a teammate's request.

- I handed the requirements to engineering at the end of August, when I moved to VinFast.

<Section id="learned" title="Time on site paid for itself">

- Three things we saw in one visit, the scooter round, the evidence rule and the charger lights, changed the requirement more than any meeting.
- A requirement is finished when an engineer can test it. That's why every story ends in acceptance criteria instead of a description.

</Section>
